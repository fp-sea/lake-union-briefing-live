Practice conditions forecast

Each row says which of the three it is: a forecast for your window, an observation that has no forecast at all, or beyond what that source reaches. The sources end at different times — the point forecast and the model run about two days out, HRRR's lightning eighteen hours, the EPA's UV around nineteen, and water temperature is never forecast — so a window far enough ahead will be answered by some rows and not others. Which rows go quiet is itself part of the answer.

No score, and no go/no-go. Wind is shown from both sources separately because where they disagree is the thing worth knowing, and a single number would be the one that hides it. Both wind rows describe Lake Union and the Ship Canal; the other two domains are not given their own rows, but the windiest of them is named whenever it runs at least 2 mph above home — which at dawn it often does, the open lake being the exposed one.

The water, and where these numbers come from

Show the map — domain outlines, forecast points, stations and the model grid

This map shows where every number on the page is sampled from. The shaded outlines are the areas the model statistics are averaged over and the areas stations are selected from; how much land falls inside each one matters, because at a 3 km model grid no reading here is purely over water.

Read the grid. The faint quadrilaterals are real HRRR cells, 3.0 km across, drawn from the model's own published projection. Filled ones are sampled for a domain; the dot is the cell center, which is what decides membership. Each domain is everything within a set distance of the line down the middle of its waterway — Lake Union 900 m, Union Bay 700 m, Open lake 900 m — wide on purpose, because at this cell size no reading here is ever purely over water. Union Bay is narrower than the other two because the bay itself is small, and a wider corridor would just spill over Webster Point into the open lake this domain exists to keep separate. That is what the water percentages measure, and it is why a domain-mean wind from this page is wind over this basin and its built-up surroundings rather than idealized open water.

Near misses, shown dotted. Cell (281,954) is 20% inside a domain and 73% water but its center falls outside, so it is not sampled, Cell (278,955) is 11% inside a domain and 0% water but its center falls outside, so it is not sampled. Drawn because with only a few cells each one matters, and a wet cell sitting just outside is usually a sign the domain is the wrong shape rather than the rule being too strict — that is exactly how the Union Bay domain was found to be missing the water north of the bay.

The cell counts matter here. lake union has 4 cells, union bay has 0 cells, lake washington has 2 cells. That is far too few for a “mean and spread” to be a distribution, so §7's domain statistics are built by sampling the model along each corridor rather than by averaging these few native cells.

Union Bay has no cell of its own — no grid center falls inside it at all. Union Bay is about 1.5 km across against a 3.0 km grid, so the model simply does not resolve the bay separately. Its numbers are interpolated from the cells around it, which is a real forecast for that spot but a smoothed one: expect it to read as a blend of the ship canal and the open lake rather than as an independent measurement of the bay.

Sun & twilight

Civil twilight is the launch-and-land decision — enough ambient light to see and be seen by other traffic. Nautical twilight is shown as the darker bound, when the horizon is no longer distinguishable. There is no astronomical twilight row: it has no navigational meaning on a lake. ("Marine twilight" is not a standard term; what people usually mean by it is nautical.)

Current conditions

No public weather station sits on this water. The nearest is 0.7 km away, at the NWS office on Sand Point. Everything below is therefore near this corridor rather than on it, and each row says how far. That distance bounds how much any of these readings can say about conditions at the dock.

“Feels like” is wind chill or heat index by the standard NWS formulas, and is shown only when conditions are actually in range for one — in this climate that is rarely, so most of the year it equals the air temperature and says so rather than implying an adjustment was made. Each reading's age is its station's observation time: these feeds publish one timestamp per observation rather than one per instrument, so every field from a station shares an age.

Point forecasts human-supervised

This is the official forecast, and the only human one on this page. Forecasters at NWS Seattle begin from model guidance — several models and their ensembles, not one — and then edit the grid themselves against local knowledge, the observations coming in, and what the neighboring offices are forecasting across the boundary. They amend it between scheduled issues when they think the models have it wrong.
Model outlook below is raw HRRR, sampled and reduced by this page and supervised by nobody. Where the two disagree, this is the one that carries a forecaster's name behind it — which is not the same as being right, but is the difference worth knowing when you choose.

Model outlook

HRRR alone for phase 1 — the only source that is simultaneously fine enough resolution, genuinely independent dynamics, and has mature subsetting today. RRFS joins once its subsetting resolves. These are domain statistics, not a point forecast and not a map: the model field is sampled across each whole domain and reduced to the number that matters for that variable.

Each domain includes the land inside it at this resolution — read these as wind over this basin and its built-up surroundings, not idealized open water. The map shows how much of each domain is actually wet.

Same three-panel layout as the point forecasts above, so the two can be read against each other directly — this is the model's view of a whole basin, that is the Weather Service's view of one spot in it.
The band is the 10th to 90th percentile across the domain, not outright min and max: with a few hundred spatially-correlated samples the extremes are single interpolation artifacts, and a band drawn to them describes noise. The heavy dashed line is the peak gust anywhere in the domain — one bad gust anywhere on this water is the number that matters — and the fine dashed line is the average gust, so the gap between them shows how much of the domain is actually near the peak rather than leaving it to be assumed uniform.
Arrows fly with the wind, pointing the way the air is moving, as on every weather map and marine app — a southerly points up the page. The tables state the direction the wind comes from, in words, because that is how a forecast is spoken; the two are the same fact 180° apart, and the N mark in the gutter beside the arrow row is there so it is never a guess which one you are reading. Their opacity is the mean resultant length R, the spread statistic for circular data: a faint arrow means the domain's samples disagree about direction, and a single confident arrow would be a lie.

The dashed line is the whole column overhead, which the model publishes separately as a loading in mg/m². It is here because a surface field alone misses the case that matters most at dawn: a plume riding above a shallow marine layer, orange sky and clean air at the dock. That smoke is not absent, it is early — when the day's mixed layer grows into it, it comes down.

It is drawn not in mg/m² on a second axis, but as the surface concentration it would become if it mixed down, so both lines share one scale and the same EPA rules. That conversion is only unit arithmetic — through a 1 km mixed layer, one mg/m² of column smoke is one µg/m³ at the surface — divided by the model's own hourly mixing height. The gap between the lines is the reading: they meet when the air is well mixed, and the dashed one rides above when the column is holding something the surface has not seen. The depth is floored at 500 m, because HRRR's boundary layer collapses to a hundred-odd meters overnight and dividing a whole column by a nocturnal layer gets the answer exactly backwards — that shallow stable layer is precisely what is holding the smoke up.

How these are sampled. Area-weighted regridding, not downscaling: the coarse model field resampled onto the water's actual footprint. It buys a better mean and adds no information. In detail, the lattice is REGULAR and not random — a 400 m grid, keeping the points that fall inside the domain, each one a distance-weighted blend of the four model cells around it, rather than an average over the cells whose centers fall inside. So the sample count is not a sample size: Lake Union's 124 lattice values carry the information of the 16 cells they are built from, and nothing more. The MEAN is the half that gains. Only 4 of those 16 cells have centers over Lake Union, 2 over the open lake and none at all over Union Bay, and the median center sits about 3 km out — so a plain average across cells would be an average of Puget Sound and Lake Washington as much as of this corridor. Weighting by where the water actually is fixes that. The BAND does not gain. Its edges land on those few on-water values: checked across several forecast hours, the 90th percentile of all 124 lattice points came within 0.02 m/s of the windiest on-water cell every time, so it is those few numbers plus the shape of the water. Read it as how much the model tilts across this water — not as uncertainty, not as gustiness, and not as downscaling. Nothing here adds detail from terrain, roughness or observations, which is what it would take to know more than the cells know.

What this technique is, and why it works time-lagged ensemble

A weather model run once gives one answer and no way to judge it. The standard way to get a confidence signal is an ensemble: run the model many times from slightly different starting conditions and see how far the answers drift apart. Where they agree the atmosphere is in a predictable state; where they scatter it is not, and the single number you would otherwise have quoted was never as solid as it looked. NOAA runs true ensembles — the GEFS, and the HRRR's own HRRRE — but they are coarser, or not published in a form this page can subset for one small corridor.

What is used here is the cheap approximation, and it has a name: a time-lagged ensemble, sometimes a lagged-average forecast. Instead of many runs initialized at one time, take the successive runs the model has already produced — HRRR initializes every hour — and line them up on the hour they are all forecasting. The 08:00 run and the 13:00 run both say something about 20:00 tonight, from five hours apart of data assimilation. If they say the same thing, that hour has been stable across five successive updates. If they disagree, the forecast for it has been moving, and it may move again.

The trade is honest and worth stating. A true ensemble varies the initial conditions deliberately, so its spread is a designed sample of the uncertainty. A lagged one varies them only by the accident of when each run was initialized, so its members are not equally skilful — the oldest is genuinely worse, having had five hours less observation to work from — and its spread will typically understate the real uncertainty. Successive runs of one model also share that model's biases, so a systematic error looks like perfect agreement. Read wide spread as a warning and narrow spread as weak reassurance, not as a probability.

For rowing the useful question is narrower than a forecast. It is not "will it be 11 mph" but "has this hour been holding still". A dawn slot that six consecutive runs have put between 7 and 9 is a slot you can plan; one they have moved between 5 and 15 is one to look at again in the morning, whatever the latest number happens to say.

Where a run stops, it stops. Each cycle reaches 6 hours from its own initialization, so the 6 runs end at 6 different times and the number covering an hour falls away to the right of every chart here. Those gaps are left as gaps — never interpolated, never carried forward — and the falling sample count is shown rather than smoothed, because an hour that only one run reaches has no agreement to report. A single value is drawn in its own neutral color for that reason: it is not perfect agreement, it is no evidence.

25 nautical miles is not an arbitrary ring. Lightning strikes up to about ten miles from the storm core, getting off this water takes twenty to thirty minutes from mid-lake, and convection here moves at 20–30 kt — so a cell 21 nm out is the one that can still reach a crew that decides now. It is rounded up to 25 because this is a forecast rather than a detection: HRRR places cells with an error of 20–30 km, and a ring drawn at exactly the decision distance would miss storms the model put slightly off. Instability — the ingredient underneath

Two fields, always together. CAPE is how much energy a rising parcel of air would gain; CIN is the cap it has to break through to get at it. Either alone misleads in the same direction — a capped 900 J/kg afternoon and an uncapped one are completely different days and read identically if only the energy is shown.
Both are the maximum within the same 25 nautical miles the lightning flag uses, so "unstable here" means the same here. The cap boundaries are Stull's (Practical Meteorology, Table 14-4) rather than anything invented here.
More cap is not simply worse, which is the part worth knowing. Under 20 J/kg the energy releases as it arrives, in scattered weak convection that never accumulates. Between 20 and 60 is the most favorable — Stull's own note is "best conditions" — because the lid holds long enough for real energy to build and still breaks when something lifts the air. Only past 60 does it start needing an exceptionally strong trigger, and past 100 it usually wins.
That non-monotonic shape is the second reason there is no single combined number here. An index would have to rise or fall consistently with each input, and this one does neither — and there is no standard CAPE-and-CIN scalar to borrow. The composite parameters that do fold CIN in are tuned for Plains supercells and would be nonsense over a lake in Seattle.
This does not set the lightning level and does not appear in the glance strip. It is an ingredient: instability is necessary for convection and nowhere near sufficient, and most of the hours below with real CAPE on them will pass without a thing happening.

Puget Sound convergence zone

Flow off the Pacific splits around the Olympics and meets again over the Sound, pinned there by the Cascades. Where the two branches collide the air has nowhere to go but up, and a narrow east–west band of heavy showers sets up with quiet, sinking air on either side of it. It is the most locally distinctive thing the weather here does, and a point forecast for this corridor cannot see it: the band is often a few miles north of the Ship Canal, which means sun on this water and hail in Lynnwood.

There is no likelihood score here, deliberately. The obvious design is a number out of wind direction, speed and season. Mass (1981) is the foundational study and says the zone is skilfully forecast from surface coastal winds — but neither it nor what followed publishes cut points, and the descriptive sources are explicit that the zone is variable in both location and strength. Every weight in such a score would be this page's own invention wearing the clothes of a probability.
So it measures the thing instead. A convergence zone is low-level convergence, and HRRR gives the 10 m wind on a 3 km grid over the Sound, so −(∂u/∂x + ∂v/∂y) is a measurement rather than a stand-in. It also answers where, which a score never could.

Each column is one forecast hour; each row a strip of latitude across the Sound, from Tacoma at the bottom to north of Everett at the top. Blue is convergence — air arriving with nowhere to go but up. A zone shows as a band: a run of blue with sinking air above and below it, which is why the chart is a profile and not a single number. Uniform light convergence over the whole Sound is not a convergence zone, and the two would look identical in a headline figure.
The wind is smoothed to 27 km before the divergence is taken. Differentiating the raw 3 km field amplifies its noise — an earlier version of this did exactly that and reported the Olympic foothills as the strongest convergence on the map.

Exactly how the number is calculated

HRRR gives two wind components on its native 3 km grid: u, the eastward component in meters per second, and v, the northward one. Divergence is how fast air is spreading out at a point — ∂u/∂x + ∂v/∂y, the rate u changes as you move east plus the rate v changes as you move north. Negate it and you have convergence: air arriving faster than it leaves, which over a surface has nowhere to go but up.
In practice, per forecast hour:

  1. u and v are each box-averaged over a 27 km window — 9 grid cells square.
  2. ∂u/∂x is a centered difference between the cells either side, divided by the 3 km spacing; ∂v/∂y is the same going north.
  3. The two are added and the sign flipped. Units are inverse seconds; the values here are shown as ×10−5 s−1 because that is the order the atmosphere works at.
  4. Within each 0.1° strip of latitude — about 11 km — the result is averaged across the Sound, from -122.75° to -122.05°. A zone is an east–west band, so averaging along it is signal; averaging across it would be what destroys the signal.

On resolution, honestly. A convergence zone band is typically 10 to 40 km wide. The 0.1° strips resolve that comfortably, but the 27 km smoothing is at the coarse end of it and will blur a narrow, sharp band into a broader, weaker one. That is a deliberate trade: at 15 km the band-to-band roughness of this profile was nearly double, and structure that moved by 10 km per hour was indistinguishable from noise. What this chart is good at is telling you a band exists and roughly where; it is not good at telling you the band is exactly 3 miles wide.

How the coastal flow is worked out, and what is this page's own choice in it

Same HRRR file, same 10 m wind. The mean of every grid cell in an offshore box — -124.9° to -124.2° longitude, 46.9° to 48.1° latitude, out in the Pacific west of the Olympics — turned into a bearing the wind comes from. That box is upwind of the split, which is where Mass says the useful predictor lives.
Two honest caveats.

  1. “West through north-west” is taken here as 260–340°, and those two numbers are this page's, not the literature's. Mass describes the regime in words and publishes no bearings. The count above is reported beside the measurement rather than multiplied into it, which keeps the stakes low — but it is still a cut point, and an unlabelled cut point is exactly what this section refuses elsewhere.
  2. The box only gets to answer when it agrees with itself. Averaging 800 cells produces a direction whether or not they point the same way. On the first hour tested the per-cell bearings ran from 10° to 323° with a circular R of 0.17 — the mean of a nearly random set, whose components largely cancelled — and the earlier version of this reported “from 60°” with no more hesitation than it would a uniform gale. Hours below R 0.6 or under 2 m/s are now counted as no coherent flow rather than as “not in the regime”, because those are different statements and only one of them is evidence.

Other hazards

Sun & UV

Exposure on the water differs from exposure ashore in three ways: there is no shade for the length of an outing, the surface reflects part of the dose upward, and a crew faces one direction throughout, so any sunburn tends to be asymmetric.

At the peak, unprotected fair skin reddens in roughly 45 minutes, which is shorter than a typical outing. Apply sunscreen to the backs of the hands, the neck and the face; open water offers no shade and reflects part of the dose upward.
